Godward content report · 9 November 2026

Draw the Old Testament's greatest stories

What Christian content gets watched on YouTube, and what Godward should make first, based on 24 ranked channels and 73 BibleProject book videos.

Make English, hand-drawn-style Old Testament story videos for teens and adults.

Stories out-perform prayer and Bible study among US-based channels. The Old Testament out-performs the New. The whiteboard "drawn and explained" format has built a 738K-subscriber channel on 139 videos. Nobody in this sample combines that format with dramatic, character-led storytelling as its main offer, and that's the opening.

1

Why "drawn and explained"

The format is digital whiteboard animation with voiceover. Its leading example is Bible Animations, based in Australia: 738K subscribers, 55M views and 139 videos in about four years, or roughly one upload every ten days.

The format beats the US story average. It's also likely simpler to produce than the documentary and full-illustration work of the two channels above it, though I have no cost data to confirm that. The strongest titles promise a complete, visual map of something mysterious:

What the channel makes

Its six main topic clusters are deeper meanings in Bible stories (15 videos), controversial questions (14), Satan, demons and spiritual warfare (12), end times (11), drawn-and-explained mega-overviews (10) and the life of Paul (6). Most of the catalog is theology and lore, so a story-first channel in this style wouldn't be competing with it directly.

Why Bible stories

Worldwide, prayer gets the most views, but that's driven by Brazilian channels posting thousands of Portuguese videos. Bispo Bruno Leonardo alone has 12.84B views. Among US-based channels, the order reverses.

US search demand agrees, with a catch. On Google Trends for US YouTube search, "prayer" alone beats "bible story" easily, but that word also catches non-Christian searches. Against specifically Christian prayer phrases, Bible stories lead 78.0 to 42.7, with Bible study at 33.9.

How long should they be?

The evidence points two ways. BibleProject's book overviews run mostly 7–9 minutes and hold 4.3M views per video, and its 5–7 minute Torah Series earns about 621K views per minute. But Bible Animations' recent uploads average about 27 minutes, and its "entire" overviews run for over an hour.

Recommended test: make the first six as standalone 6–9 minute stories, then stitch them into one 30–45 minute compilation. That tests long-form viewing at almost no extra cost. Let your own retention numbers decide from there.

5

How to make them work

Do

  • Tell the story with characters and tension, not book structure.
  • Lead with a mystery, a conflict or an unusual point of view.
  • Make each video stand on its own.
  • Aim for one upload every 7–10 days.
  • Review retention after the first 5–8 videos.

Avoid

  • Two-part videos: Part 1 beat Part 2 in every pair checked.
  • Short letters early on.
  • English prayer as the main format.
  • Copying BibleProject's book-summary approach.
  • Clickbait the story can't back up, which costs trust.

Kids or adults?

Aim for teens and adults. Kids' channels get strong views per subscriber, but repeat viewing may inflate those numbers, and YouTube limits ads and comments on content made for children.

A realistic money picture

OutlierKit estimates Bible Animations earns about $2K–$6K a month including sponsorship, after four years and 738K subscribers. That's a third-party estimate rather than reported income, but it's a useful check on expectations.

A good second format

Once the channel has traction, try Bible study. US study channels drew nearly as many recent views as story channels (16.0M against 19.6M), though that category depends on viewers trusting a specific teacher.

6

How far to trust this

  • Small, hand-picked sample. These channels were selected by hand, so the report shows what has worked for them and can't guarantee the same for a new channel.
  • One channel carries the format case. Bible Animations is the only drawn-and-explained channel measured, and it's Australian, so it isn't part of the US averages. Similar channels such as The Drawn Bible weren't analyzed.
  • "US" means where a channel is registered. Those channels' views include viewers worldwide.
  • Book popularity comes from one channel. Reading data points elsewhere: Psalms is often cited as the most-read book but ranks 21st by video views.
  • The story category skews young. Three of the four US story channels are aimed largely at children, which may inflate their averages.
  • Numbers are approximate. Channel figures come from third-party trackers, and YouTube rounds view counts. Bible Animations' figures are from July 2026, the others from November 2026.
  • No retention data. Public numbers don't show how long people watch, which is what will decide length and pacing.
