# No Carved Image: full script

The Ten Commandments, part 2: the second commandment. Exodus 20:4–6, with Deuteronomy 4 and Isaiah 44. Working title: *What's Wrong With a Statue of God?*

**Runtime:** about 7¾ minutes (≈1,030 words including the scripture readings, at 140–145 words a minute, plus pauses).
**Look:** the glowing-collage style of `videos/creation-and-the-fall/6-vox-kurzgesagt-mix.mp4`, the same as episode 1 and part 1.
**Voice:** one narrator, calm and curious, with short pauses before the turns.
**Translation:** English Standard Version (ESV). See "Scripture text" below.

## Thesis and spine

**Thesis:** An image of God makes him too small and too quiet. At Sinai, God gave Israel words to listen to, and he had already given the world people to reflect him.

**Misconception it overturns:** that this commandment is about other religions' idols, or just repeats the first. Named in the cold open, paid off in section 2.

**Spine:** Isaiah mocks a man who worships half a log, BUT the question behind the joke is serious, THEREFORE we go back to Sinai, where Israel saw no form. BUT God then forbids images of anything in creation, AND YET he tells Moses to carve stone, THEREFORE the line is between carving words and carving a face. BUT why does that matter? Because an image shrinks God and silences him. BUT God already has an image on earth: people. AND YET we still carve, THEREFORE the commandment ends with a warning outweighed by mercy.

**Re-hooks:** 2:05 ("Wait. Isn't that the same as the first commandment?"), 3:55 ("So why does that matter so much?"), 5:00 ("And here's where it gets interesting"), 6:55 ("So if we're all still carving…").

## How this episode is built

This one is a **teaching episode**, where part 1 was a story episode. Its shape follows the pattern studied in BibleProject's first-commandment video, rebuilt for a single narrator and Godward's own content:

1. **Hook.** A strange, concrete picture (Godward addition: BibleProject opens on context).
2. **Setting.** Where the words were spoken and who heard them.
3. **The verse,** read in full.
4. **"Notice."** One detail in the text that's easy to miss.
5. **One word, looked at closely,** with a second passage that uses the same word.
6. **A connection** to another part of the Bible.
7. **Why it matters,** with concrete modern examples.
8. **Closing turn** that answers the hook and reframes the command as a gift.

Where the video uses a second host to check in and sum up, this script uses a rhetorical question and a pause.

## Style rules for every scene

Same as `01-creation-and-the-fall.md` and `ten-commandments-1-no-other-gods.md`, with these additions:

- **The carpenter.** A round figure like the others, in a workshop sheet of warm wood-brown paper with curled wood shavings as cut-outs.
- **Images and idols.** Gold-foil and carved-wood cut-outs with a hard shine and no glow of their own, as with the calf in part 1.
- **People.** In section 6, every human figure on screen carries a soft glow. This is the visual answer to the episode's question.
- **God.** Never shown as a person, and in this episode especially so. God's presence is fire, cloud and light, and God's speech appears as words on paper inside that light.
- **Scripture.** As in part 1: torn cream strips, a black reference label, followed by "ESV".
- **Pause markers.** As in part 1: `[beat]` 0.3 s, `[pause]` 0.8 s, `[hold]` 1.5 s, on their own narration line, never spoken.

Scripture is quoted exactly from the ESV. Everything else is paraphrase and is never presented as scripture.

## 0. Cold open: half a log (0:00–0:55)

**Visual:** The workshop sheet. A carpenter splits a log in two. One half goes into a small fire; he warms his hands and roasts food over it. The other half goes on the bench, and he carves it into a figure. Then he kneels in front of it. The fire is still burning beside him. A coral marker draws an arrow from one half to the other.

**On screen:**
- {{ESV Isaiah 44:16}} (keep the first clause, about burning half of it) · ISAIAH 44:16 ESV
- {{ESV Isaiah 44:17}} (keep his prayer at the end of the verse) · ISAIAH 44:17 ESV
- Black label: **SAME TREE.**

**Narration:**
> A man cuts down a tree. Half of it, he burns. He cooks his dinner on it, warms his hands, and says, Aha, I'm warm. The other half, he carves into a statue. Then he kneels down in front of it, and he prays to it. Save me, he says. You are my god.

> Same tree. One half is firewood. The other half is his saviour.

> The prophet Isaiah tells that story as a joke. But it's a joke about a commandment. And the question behind it is a serious one: what's actually wrong with a statue of God?

> Most people read this commandment as a ban on other religions' idols. But that's mostly the first commandment's job. This one is aimed somewhere closer to home.

**Transition:** The camera slides right to a charcoal sheet: Sinai, wrapped in cloud.

## 1. A voice, and nothing else (0:55–1:40)

**Visual:** The Sinai sheet from part 1. Fire on the summit, cloud, neon lightning. The people stand behind the boundary line. The camera pushes into the fire, searching. There's no shape inside it, only light and words forming on paper. A coral marker draws an empty dotted outline in the fire, with a "?" inside.

**On screen:** {{ESV Deuteronomy 4:12}} · DEUTERONOMY 4:12 ESV

**Narration:**
> To answer that, go back to the mountain. Israel has just been rescued from Egypt, and now they're standing at the foot of Sinai while God speaks out of the fire.

> Later, Moses reminds them what that day was like:

> {{ESV Deuteronomy 4:12}}

> Think about that. The most important meeting in their history, and nobody saw a thing. No face. No figure. Just fire, and words.

> And according to Moses, that wasn't an accident.

## 2. The second word (1:40–2:30)

**Visual:** A torn strip slides across the fire and the highlighter sweeps "carved image". Then a second strip arrives below it.

**On screen:**
- {{ESV Exodus 20:4}} · EXODUS 20:4 ESV
- {{ESV Exodus 20:5}} (keep the first sentence, about bowing down and serving them) · EXODUS 20:5 ESV

**Narration:**
> Right after the command to have no other gods, God says this:

> {{ESV Exodus 20:4}}

> And then:

> {{ESV Exodus 20:5}} (read the first sentence only)

> Wait. Isn't that the same as the first commandment?

> [beat]

> Not quite. The first commandment is about which God. The second is about how. Even the right God can be worshipped the wrong way.

> A quick note: Christians number the commandments differently, and some traditions count this as part of the first. Either way, the point is the same.

## 3. Notice the list (2:30–3:10)

**Visual:** The strip stays pinned. Below it, three paper panels open like a field notebook: SKY (birds, sun, stars), LAND (cattle, creeping things), WATER (fish). Each panel lights up as it's named. Then the camera pulls back and the three panels slide next to the six-day grid from episode 1, where the same creatures sit in the same order. Coral brackets join the two.

**On screen:** Black label: **HEAVEN · EARTH · WATER = THE CREATION WEEK**

**Narration:**
> Notice the list. Heaven above. The earth beneath. The water under the earth. It's not random. It's the whole world, the way Genesis lays it out: sky, land, sea, and everything God filled them with.

> So the commandment isn't only banning a few strange idols. It's saying: nothing in creation can stand in for the Creator. Not the sun. Not a bull. Not the strongest, most beautiful thing you've ever seen.

> Everything God made points to him. None of it is him.

## 4. One word: carved (3:10–4:00)

**Visual:** The word CARVED on a torn strip, enlarged. Its Hebrew root appears beside it in a black label box: **פסל · P-S-L · "TO CARVE, TO CUT"**. The camera slides to a new sheet: Moses on the mountainside, chiselling two blank stone tablets. A second strip arrives, and coral marker circles "Cut" and joins it to "carved" with an arrow.

**On screen:**
- {{ESV Exodus 34:1}} (keep the first instruction, about cutting two tablets) · EXODUS 34:1 ESV
- Black label: **SAME ROOT. DIFFERENT PURPOSE.**

**Narration:**
> Now look closely at one word: "carved". In Hebrew, it comes from a verb that means to cut or to carve. A carved image is something you've cut out with your own hands.

> And that same verb turns up again a few chapters later, in a surprising place. After the golden calf, after the first tablets are smashed, God tells Moses to cut two new tablets of stone, like the first ones.

> Same verb. Moses is told to carve stone. But not into a face. Into a surface for words.

> That's the whole difference. Israel is allowed to carve stone for God's words. They're not allowed to carve stone into God's face.

> So why does that matter so much?

## 5. A god that holds still (4:00–5:00)

**Visual:** A row of gold and wooden idols on a shelf. As each body part is named, a coral marker circles it on one idol: the mouth, the eyes, the ears, the hands, the feet. Each circle gets a black X. Then the carpenter's figure from the cold open is shown, still kneeling, still waiting.

**On screen:** {{ESV Psalm 115:5}} · PSALM 115:5 ESV

**Narration:**
> Because an image does two things. It makes God smaller, and it makes God quieter.

> Smaller, because whatever you carve, it's less than the one who carved the universe. And quieter, because a statue only ever says what you put into it. A psalm puts it plainly:

> {{ESV Psalm 115:5}}

> An image holds still. It stays where you put it. You can carry it, manage it, take it out when you need it. And that's the appeal. A God you can see is a God you can control.

> The God of Sinai doesn't hold still. He speaks. And a God who speaks can tell you something you didn't want to hear.

## 6. The image God already made (5:00–5:50)

**Visual:** A soft dissolve to the Day 6 panel from episode 1: two glowing figures. The torn strip from Genesis lands beside them and the highlighter sweeps "image". The camera pulls back, and the sheet fills with people: the crowd from Sinai, then figures of every shape and age, each one with a glow.

**On screen:** {{ESV Genesis 1:27}} · GENESIS 1:27 ESV

**Narration:**
> And here's where it gets interesting. The Bible isn't against God having an image in the world. It just says he's already made one.

> Go back to the very first chapter:

> {{ESV Genesis 1:27}}

> Not a statue. Not a carving. People.

> If you want to see something that reflects God on earth, the Bible doesn't point you to gold or wood. It points you to the person next to you. That's why a carved image isn't only an insult to God. It's a replacement for something he already put here.

## 7. The images we still carve (5:50–7:00)

**Visual:** The workshop sheet again, but the carpenter is now carving abstract shapes: a coin with a halo, a flag with a crown, a smiling mask that looks exactly like the carpenter. Each one is gold foil with no glow. A coral marker writes beside the last one: **GOD, BUT AGREES WITH ME.**

**On screen:** {{ESV Acts 17:29}} · ACTS 17:29 ESV

**Narration:**
> Christians have argued about images for centuries. Catholic and Orthodox churches use icons, but they draw a sharp line: an image can be honoured as something that points to God, and never worshipped as God. On that line, every tradition agrees.

> But most of us will never carve a statue anyway. We still carve. Just not with wood.

> We carve a God who always approves of whatever we wanted anyway. A God who's on our side in every argument. A God who exists to keep us comfortable, and goes quiet when we'd rather not hear.

> Those images are easier to live with. They're also smaller than the real thing. Centuries later, Paul would tell a crowd in Athens:

> {{ESV Acts 17:29}}

> That includes the ones we carve in our heads.

## 8. A thousand generations (7:00–7:40)

**Visual:** Back to the commandment strip. The camera slides along its second half. Coral marker circles "third and fourth" and writes **4** beside it, then circles "thousands" and writes **1,000s**. The second circle is much bigger. Then the final shot: the empty fire on Sinai, and words forming inside it.

**On screen:** {{ESV Exodus 20:6}} · EXODUS 20:6 ESV

**Narration:**
> So if we're all still carving, where does that leave us?

> [pause]

> The commandment ends with a warning and a promise. The damage of turning away echoes for three or four generations. But the mercy runs to thousands of those who love him.

> Four, against thousands. The command is heavy. The mercy is heavier.

> So what's wrong with a statue of God? It's too small, and it's too quiet. Israel saw no shape on the mountain because God wasn't giving them something to look at. He was giving them someone to listen to.

## 9. End card (7:40–7:50)

**Visual:** The camera pulls back to show the whole wall: the carpenter, the fire on Sinai, the carved tablets, the shelf of idols, the glowing crowd. The grain settles and the sheets dim to black.

**On screen:** Series title card, then **NEXT: THE NAME**. Then the ESV copyright notice.

**Narration:** none (music only).

## Notes for production

- **Translation and permission.** All scripture is from the ESV, which is under copyright to Crossway. See the same note in part 1: show the copyright notice on screen and in the description, and check Crossway's and the ESV API's current terms before the channel is monetised.
- **Words the script relies on.** Section 4 assumes the ESV renders Exodus 20:4 with "carved image" and Exodus 34:1 with "Cut". Section 8 assumes Exodus 20:5–6 contrast "third and fourth generation" with "thousands". Check the filled text and adjust the highlighted words to match if they differ.
- **Isaiah 44.** The cold open paraphrases Isaiah 44:15–17, and the strips quote 44:16 and 44:17. "Aha, I'm warm" and "Save me, you are my god" are the narrator's paraphrase.
- **"Carved" and "cut".** Exodus 20:4's "carved image" is *pesel*, from the verb *pasal*, "to hew, to carve". Exodus 34:1's instruction to cut the tablets (and Deuteronomy 10:1) uses the same verb, *pesal-lekha*. The script claims only the shared verb.
- **"Notice the list."** The link between Exodus 20:4's heaven-earth-water list and the creation week is an interpretive reading, presented as such ("the way Genesis lays it out"). Deuteronomy 4:16–19 spells out the same range of creatures and heavenly bodies.
- **"He's already made one."** Reading Genesis 1:27 against the image ban is a common interpretation in Jewish and Christian teaching. Genesis uses a different Hebrew word for "image" (*tselem*) from Exodus 20:4 (*pesel*); the script makes a thematic connection, not a word-for-word one.
- **"Four, against thousands."** "Thousands" can be read as thousands of people or thousands of generations (compare Deuteronomy 7:9, "a thousand generations"). The script's contrast holds on either reading.
- **Icons.** Section 7's line about Catholic and Orthodox icons follows the distinction those churches draw between honour or veneration given to images and worship given to God alone (the Second Council of Nicaea, 787). Protestant traditions differ on whether images belong in worship at all; the script states only the point all of them share.
- **Commandment numbering.** As in part 1, the script follows the Reformed and Orthodox numbering, where Exodus 20:4–6 is the second commandment. In Catholic and Lutheran numbering it belongs to the first. The end card's "THE NAME" (Exodus 20:7) is the third commandment in this numbering.
- **Relationship to the BibleProject video.** Only the episode's shape is modelled on that video: setting, verse, a "notice" moment, a word study, a connection, application, closing turn. The wording, the commandment, the examples and every word study here are Godward's own.
