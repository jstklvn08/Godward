# The Creation and Fall of Man: script

Genesis 1:26–3:24, told by one narrator as an idea-led explainer, in the visual style of `videos/creation-and-the-fall/6-vox-kurzgesagt-mix.mp4`.

Its approach was informed by BibleProject's "Book of Genesis Summary: A Complete Animated Overview (Part 1)": read the fall as a choice about who defines good and evil, then follow the damage. The wording and visuals here are original. Curly quotation marks are used only for exact King James text.

**Carried over from the full script:** God is never drawn, only light and glowing words. The serpent is only labeled THE SERPENT. She is labeled THE WOMAN until Scene 10. The characters wear simple clothing throughout.

**One visual idea runs through the film:** a large paper card labeled GOOD. In Scenes 1–4 only a beam of light can stamp it. In Scene 8, a human hand takes the stamp.

---

## Scene 1: The question

**Palette:** near-black paper.
**Visual:** A blank card labeled GOOD sits alone on a dark wall, under a single light. A red marker writes a large question mark beside it. A black label appears: WHO DECIDES?

> Who gets to decide what is good? It sounds like a question for a philosophy class. But according to the opening pages of the Bible, it's the question that broke the world.

## Scene 2: Made to represent

**Palette:** indigo warming to teal and gold.
**Visual:** Fast montage of glowing creation on the paper wall, with the GOOD stamp landing seven times, counted by a black label. Two chubby figures appear in a circle of light. A halftone cut-out of an ancient king's statue is taped next to them, joined by a dotted red line. Tiny figures then spread across a map of the earth, each with a small glow.
**Strip:** “Let us make man in our image, after our likeness: and let them have dominion” (Genesis 1:26)

> Genesis opens with God bringing order out of darkness and filling the world with life. Seven times in the first chapter, he looks at what he's made and calls it good. Then he makes something different. “Let us make man in our image, after our likeness: and let them have dominion.” In the ancient world, a king's image stood in for the king. It showed who was in charge. So humans aren't an afterthought. They're placed on the earth to represent God, and to care for it the way he would.

## Scene 3: Earthling

**Palette:** dark earth with one thread of white light.
**Visual:** Two paper strips slide together, reading ADAM and ADAMAH, and yellow highlighter sweeps the letters they share. Labels read HUMAN and GROUND. Dust rises and gathers into a figure, a thread of light enters, and the dot eyes open.
**Strip:** “And the LORD God formed man of the dust of the ground” (Genesis 2:7)

> Chapter two zooms in. “And the LORD God formed man of the dust of the ground.” There's a pun here that gets lost in English. The Hebrew word for human is adam. The word for ground is adamah. We are, quite literally, earthlings. Then God breathes his own breath into that dirt, “and man became a living soul.” Made from the lowest thing there is, and filled with the life of God.

## Scene 4: Two trees

**Palette:** bioluminescent Eden teal.
**Visual:** Eden opens through a torn-paper window. Two great trees are labeled. The tree of life glows steadily. On the other tree, the GOOD card hangs from a branch, and a beam of light holds the stamp. A yellow arrow labeled THE CHOICE points between the man and the tree.
**Strips:** “thou shalt not eat of it: for in the day that thou eatest thereof thou shalt surely die.” (Genesis 2:17)

> God puts the man in a garden, to work it and keep it. Every tree is his to enjoy, except one: the tree of the knowledge of good and evil. “Thou shalt not eat of it: for in the day that thou eatest thereof thou shalt surely die.” Why plant a tree and then forbid it? Many readers see that tree as a place of decision. So far, God has been the one who says what is good. Now humans face a real choice. Will they trust his word about what is good, or take that decision for themselves? And beside it stands the other tree, the tree of life. Turning from God means turning from the source of life itself.

## Scene 5: Not good

**Palette:** teal warming to rose.
**Visual:** The man stands alone. The GOOD stamp hovers, then a red marker writes NOT GOOD. A second figure appears, labeled THE WOMAN. Strips reading ISH and ISHSHAH slide together. Their two auras merge into one, and the last strip of the scene stays pinned at the right edge of the frame.
**Strips:** “It is not good that the man should be alone” (Genesis 2:18) · “This is now bone of my bones, and flesh of my flesh” (Genesis 2:23) · “And they were both naked, the man and his wife, and were not ashamed.” (Genesis 2:25)

> Then, for the first time, God says something isn't good. “It is not good that the man should be alone.” So he makes a woman, from the man's own side. The man bursts into poetry. “This is now bone of my bones, and flesh of my flesh.” And Hebrew has another pun waiting. Man is ish, and woman is ishshah. They belong together, and they have nothing to hide. “And they were both naked, the man and his wife, and were not ashamed.”

## Scene 6: The pun that changes everything

**Palette:** rose draining, with crimson bleeding in from the edges.
**Visual:** The camera slides right. The pinned strip from Scene 5 meets a new one, and yellow highlighter marks one Hebrew word on each: ARUM (NAKED) and ARUM (CRAFTY). Red marker joins them. The magenta serpent winds down into frame, between the two strips.
**Strip:** “Now the serpent was more subtil than any beast of the field” (Genesis 3:1)

> Watch how the next sentence begins. “Now the serpent was more subtil than any beast of the field.” Subtil means crafty. In Hebrew, the word for crafty sounds almost exactly like the word for naked, which was used one sentence earlier. Two people with nothing to hide, and a creature with everything to hide. Genesis says very little about who this serpent is. Only that it's a creature God made, and that it has a different story to tell.

## Scene 7: A different story

**Palette:** crimson on aged paper.
**Visual:** Two torn strips hang side by side, labeled GOD SAID and THE SERPENT SAID. The serpent's words write themselves over God's, in red. A halftone cut-out of a smiling face peels away to reveal a clenched fist. Then a black label: GOD IS HOLDING OUT ON YOU.
**Strips:** “Yea, hath God said, Ye shall not eat of every tree of the garden?” (Genesis 3:1) · “Ye shall not surely die” (Genesis 3:4) · “ye shall be as gods, knowing good and evil” (Genesis 3:5)

> It starts with a question. “Yea, hath God said, Ye shall not eat of every tree of the garden?” Then a flat denial. “Ye shall not surely die.” Then the real offer. “Ye shall be as gods, knowing good and evil.” The serpent never asks them to hate God. It only suggests that the command isn't care at all. It's God keeping the best for himself. If you believe that, the choice seems obvious.

## Scene 8: The stamp changes hands

**Palette:** full crimson, with the fruit the brightest thing on screen.
**Visual:** Two strips are compared, with SAW and GOOD highlighted on each. The GOOD card hangs in the frame. The beam of light holding the stamp fades. A small hand reaches up, and takes the stamp. It comes down on the card with a heavy thud. A shockwave ripples outward from the card, and every glow in Eden goes out.
**Strips:** “And God saw every thing that he had made, and, behold, it was very good.” (Genesis 1:31) · “And when the woman saw that the tree was good for food” (Genesis 3:6) · “she took of the fruit thereof, and did eat, and gave also unto her husband with her; and he did eat.” (Genesis 3:6)

> Now listen to how Genesis describes the moment. All through chapter one, God saw that it was good. Here, for the first time, someone else does the seeing. “And when the woman saw that the tree was good for food.” The stamp has changed hands. “She took of the fruit thereof, and did eat, and gave also unto her husband with her; and he did eat.” They got exactly what they reached for. They now decide what is good. And it costs them everything.

## Scene 9: Three fractures

**Palette:** drained crimson sliding to deep purple.
**Visual:** The merged aura splits, and a crack tears across the paper between the two figures. Leaf cut-outs are taped on. A label reads 1. EACH OTHER. A soft white light moves through the trees, and they hide. A label reads 2. GOD. Yellow arrows of blame shoot from the man to the woman to the serpent. Then the crack runs out across the ground, and black paper thorns grow from it. A label reads 3. THE WORLD.
**Strips:** “and they sewed fig leaves together, and made themselves aprons.” (Genesis 3:7) · “Where art thou?” (Genesis 3:9) · “for dust thou art, and unto dust shalt thou return.” (Genesis 3:19)

> The damage spreads in layers. First, between the two of them. People who were unashamed now cover themselves, “and they sewed fig leaves together, and made themselves aprons.” Next, between them and God. They hear him in the garden, and they hide. So God asks, “Where art thou?” What he gets in reply is blame. The man blames the woman, and God for giving her to him. The woman blames the serpent. And finally, the fracture reaches the earth itself. Work turns to struggle, the ground grows thorns, and the earthling goes back to the earth. “For dust thou art, and unto dust shalt thou return.”

## Scene 10: The promise

**Palette:** dark purple, with one small gold point of light.
**Visual:** One strip is pulled from the pile and taped in the center. A tiny gold light appears beside her. The label THE WOMAN peels away, and a new one is taped on: EVE. The crooked leaves are peeled away, and warm coats appear on the two figures, stitched with a thin gold line.
**Strips:** “it shall bruise thy head, and thou shalt bruise his heel.” (Genesis 3:15) · “And Adam called his wife's name Eve; because she was the mother of all living.” (Genesis 3:20) · “Unto Adam also and to his wife did the LORD God make coats of skins, and clothed them.” (Genesis 3:21)

> But the story doesn't end in the wreckage. Speaking to the serpent, God says that a descendant of the woman will come, and of the serpent, “it shall bruise thy head, and thou shalt bruise his heel.” The serpent will be defeated, and the one who defeats it will be wounded. Christians have long read this as the Bible's first hint of a rescuer. The man, facing death, names his wife Eve, “because she was the mother of all living.” And before they leave, God himself clothes them. “Unto Adam also and to his wife did the LORD God make coats of skins, and clothed them.”

## Scene 11: East of Eden

**Palette:** purple night, with the gold point still glowing.
**Visual:** The tree of life glows far behind them. The gate rises, with wings of light and a turning sword, as in clip 6. Two small figures walk away across the paper. The camera slides back to the GOOD card from Scene 1, now covered in thousands of conflicting stamps. The red question mark redraws itself. The gold point is the last thing to fade.
**Strip:** “to keep the way of the tree of life.” (Genesis 3:24)

> Then they're sent out, and the way back is guarded, “to keep the way of the tree of life.” Every generation since has lived with that first decision. We still reach for the stamp. We still argue over who gets to hold it. So the question from the beginning is still open. Who gets to decide what is good? And can the way back to life ever be opened again? The rest of the Bible is its answer.
